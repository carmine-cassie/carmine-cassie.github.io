What I Read this Month!

The Secret History

The obvious thing to mention with this book is its, I don't know, "aesthetic". Widely attributed as the progenitor of the Dark Academia trend, The Secret History gives us starchy shirts and wool overcoats, performative, non-prescription spectacles, and late nights spent reading the classics (in the original language, of course).

We follow an elite, tiny class of deep-pocketed Ancient Greek students, living extravagantly off of their trust funds, and this aesthetic appeals! How this book made me wish that I could spend all day learning dead languages and reading classics, never fearing I need get a job on account of my greater god: Passive Income. The characters live in a ghost-world, where they don't need to work, or interact with society, which has appealing elements to someone like me, steeped as I am in hunting for jobs I don't really want.

I was so struck by the richness of the prose, reading this. I've been reading a lot of, I don't know, web serial slop this year - not that the slop isn't often tasty, but each sentence has less care when it's written so quickly. Early in this book, some sentences took me extra moments to parse because they weren't put together in the simplest, most efficient way, but elegantly, and with care. Donna Tartt is a writer who knows how to use semicolons, which are unfamiliar territory for me as a reader! Really beautiful prose throughout, likely a product of writing 200k words in ten years rather than the 2M words in 2 years that I'm a little accustomed to. I shall have to seek out more of this.

The protagonist is very passive, he just sort of walks towards the fancy-looking things and takes the path of least resistance whenever challenged, which I didn't find particularly interesting, but I really enjoyed the rest of the cast! Henry was a particular standout for me. The plot didn't particularly grip me, it progressed as one might expect, but I suppose I was reading it for the other (excellent) aspects.

What I wasn't expecting was how much the book concerns itself with picking apart the performative nature of the dark academia aesthetic! Like, I was surprised that the online trend that feels so superficial has been that way from its conception. The characters want to appear elite, educated, a cut above, and after playing dress-up with their expensive suits and Greek textbooks for months or years, it becomes less of an act. They wind up as bona fide snobby, unpleasant people, just like they wanted. I think they're barely even friends, and that lack of connection really hurts them (hence the murder).

DNFs

I also started but did not finish two books this month!

The Burn Line

A Jonathan Sims book, we follow five Londoners who have some independent but somehow related spooky experiences! Each of the first five chapters follows a different person, and after the fifth mildly mysterious but barely interesting inciting incident in a row I was pretty bored, and not eager to have to go through five instances of each other step of the story, so I put it down!

WARHOUND: Volume One

While reading the TTRPG Girlframe and considering the prospect of running it, I wanted to immerse myself a bit in the Mechsploitation genre (into which it is an entry). Mechsploitation stories concern themselves with the toxic relationships between sci-fi mech pilots and their handlers, and, Warhound being the seminal piece that introduced the genre (as far as I can tell), I decided to give it a go!

So, Warhound is smut, it's pornographic fiction, which isn't something I've read before! I've heard about how popular Romantasy is, and I was once surprised in a bookstore by a sexy scene when flipping through a book to check the prose quality, so going into Warhound I was very curious! Pornography and sex are quite sensitive topics, so I'm second-guessing everything I write here - I want to share my thoughts and remarks on this book, but I'm cognizant that the experience it wants to cultivate in the reader is perhaps more private and less blog-suitable than what, say, Piranesi is aiming for, which maybe makes talking about my experience with it more vulnerable? Very strange.

Volume One of Warhound is a gathering of several smaller pieces: two one-shots, a three parter, and big old novel. I stopped reading a few chapters into the novel, when I found that I didn't have any desire to find out what would happen next.

There were a lot of interesting, hm, contradictions, in this book, if that's the word I'm looking for. Kallidora Rho brings great ideas, has a good understanding of sexiness and writes sex scenes quite well, I thought (as an outsider to smut). But then we have the way the book will, like, repeatedly pause to comprehensively explain the toxic relationships between the characters, which made those relationships feel thin, where they might not have been if we were simply told less about them (over and over). Perhaps it's written with the assumption that the reader will be, hm, distracted? I also found it quite amusing how, being written for lesbian audiences, it's vaguely suggested that there are men, like, around - one assumes it would be a roughly 50/50 split, but any character that gets more than half a sentence of screentime is, without fail, an attractive woman. As I kept reading I found the faith in the "lesbian suspension of disbelief" (as I termed it) increasingly silly.

I don't anticipate I'll be picking up more smut any time soon, but I'm glad to have given it a go!
